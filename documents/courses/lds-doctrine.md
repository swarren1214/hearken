# Latter-day Saint Doctrine: Novice to Sage

This course describes the Utah-based Church’s doctrine. It does not purport to represent every Latter Day Saint denomination. Confessional claims are identified as such. Historic commentary and comparison sources are labeled, and no claim of academic consensus is inferred from an institutional source.

This is the complete authored curriculum package, ready for editorial review and implementation. It is not yet installed in the app. All required work is available through original material and free reading links; paid books and videos are optional. Assessment measures understanding, evidence, and reasoning rather than assent or religious worthiness.

Each stage’s advancement check earns 0 XP and gates the next stage. A stage unlocks its earned tier only after every required activity and assessment passes. Optional video work earns 20 XP once, optional reading work 30 once; neither substitutes for required work.

Checkpoint and advancement responses are scored 0–2: missing/incorrect, partial/unsupported, or accurate/contextual. Pass at 80%; unlimited retries and no repeat XP. Required activities require a meaningful summary, sourced reading response, or completed artifact—not opening a link. Capstone scoring is described below.

Suggested workload: about 50–60 hours including readings, exercises, assessments, and capstone revision. Item time estimates are planning estimates, not validated pilot results. Learners can work at their own pace. A facilitator or validated assessment workflow is needed for written submissions.

Each module includes a reusable original article, explicit learning items, assigned readings with response prompts, and an application exercise. Answer guides are in ASSESSMENT_GUIDE.md.

### 1.1. Sources, authority, and careful claims

**Objectives**

- Distinguish canon, institutional teaching, commentary, and historical evidence.
- State a doctrinal claim with a source and a limit.

**Learning items**

- The standard works function as the course’s canonical starting point.
- Current institutional explanation, individual commentary, and historical documentation have different evidentiary roles.
- A doctrinal claim must state its source and the scope of its support.

**Original Hearken article · 20 XP**

Studying doctrine begins with identifying the kind of claim being made. A passage can be canonical, a statement can represent current institutional teaching, and a book can offer useful commentary without all three having the same function. A historical record may tell us what someone taught at a particular time without settling what the Church teaches today. Keep four fields beside every note: claim, source, setting, and confidence. Ask whether your wording is broader than the evidence. “This passage teaches…” is often more defensible than “Every Latter-day Saint believes…”. This course assesses your ability to understand and explain teachings; it does not assess personal belief or religious worthiness. Start a source ledger now. You will revise it as new evidence changes how precisely you can express a claim.

Completion: write a short summary naming one source and one interpretive limit.

### 1.2. The Godhead and the language of unity

**Objectives**

- Explain the LDS distinction between unity and identity.
- Represent Nicene belief without caricature.

**Learning items**

- LDS teaching distinguishes the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost.
- Unity of purpose should not be collapsed into identity of person.
- Comparative definitions must come from the tradition being described.

**Original Hearken article · 20 XP**

Theological words are useful only when their meanings are made explicit. In Latter-day Saint teaching, Father, Son, and Holy Ghost are distinct, while their work and purpose are united. Read statements about their unity beside passages that distinguish them. Do not turn “one” into a single definition that must apply in every context. When comparing this teaching with Nicene Christianity, ask how each tradition uses person, being, substance, and unity. Nicene belief is not simply the idea that God changes masks; that description confuses the Trinity with modalism. A good first explanation stays close to its own sources and admits where ordinary analogies break down. Make a two-column glossary: what a source says, and the meaning you are assigning to its words.

Completion: write a short summary naming one source and one interpretive limit.

### 2.1. The plan of salvation and moral agency

**Objectives**

- Connect creation, mortality, redemption, and eternal life.
- Distinguish agency from freedom from consequences.

**Learning items**

- Immortality and eternal life name the stated divine purpose in Moses 1:39.
- Christ’s redemption connects mortal life with eternal hope.
- Agency does not mean equal circumstances or freedom from consequences.

**Original Hearken article · 20 XP**

A plan-of-salvation diagram is a learning aid, not a photograph of the unseen world. Its value lies in connecting human life with divine purpose: creation, meaningful choice, Christ’s redemption, resurrection, and hope. Keep the diagram anchored in passages rather than filling every gap with speculation. Agency describes meaningful moral action; it does not imply that people choose every circumstance or have equal opportunities. Illness, coercion, poverty, and other constraints matter when discussing responsibility. A careful account of agency therefore makes room for compassion and judgment that exceeds our knowledge. Draw the broad connections first. Then label which details are stated in a source, which are reasonable interpretations, and which remain questions. This habit protects a useful model from becoming an unwarranted certainty.

### 2.2. The doctrine of Christ as a lived beginning

**Objectives**

- Identify faith, repentance, baptism, and the gift of the Holy Ghost.
- Explain why discipleship continues after an ordinance.

**Learning items**

- Faith in Jesus Christ, repentance, baptism, and receiving the Holy Ghost are foundational.
- Enduring discipleship follows entry into the path.
- Learning about a religious commitment differs from making that commitment.

**Original Hearken article · 20 XP**

An introductory course can make discipleship look like a checklist. The doctrine of Christ instead invites a continuing relationship expressed through trust, change, covenant, and life in the Spirit. Faith is directed toward Jesus Christ; repentance involves turning toward God rather than merely feeling bad. Ordinances are significant acts within that relationship, while enduring to the end describes ongoing faithfulness rather than earning a once-for-all certificate. Read for the verbs: come, repent, follow, receive, endure. Ask what each verb asks a learner to understand. Avoid grading someone’s private spiritual experience as evidence of learning. You can explain the teaching, analyze a passage, and apply it to a fictional case without disclosing a confession, testimony, or personal religious commitment.

### 3.1. Premortal life, creation, and the Fall

**Objectives**

- Read distinct creation accounts without forcing a scientific chronology.
- Explain the Fall’s place in LDS redemptive teaching.

**Learning items**

- Creation, the Fall, and redemption are connected in LDS accounts of mortality.
- Abraham 3 introduces premortal life within its own narrative setting.
- Scripture’s theological claims do not automatically determine a scientific chronology.

**Original Hearken article · 20 XP**

Creation narratives teach through structure, imagery, and theological emphasis. Read each account in its own setting before combining it with another. A passage about divine purpose does not automatically establish a mechanism, scientific date, or complete physical history. Latter-day Saint accounts connect creation and the Fall with agency, mortality, and redemption. That connection should not erase the narrative’s difficulty or make every consequence of mortality desirable. Distinguish what the text affirms, what an institutional explanation adds, and what a modern reader supplies. Comparison is especially useful when one account emphasizes order and another emphasizes relationship or choice. Your task is to explain those emphases with textual evidence, while leaving scientific and historical claims at the level their evidence supports.

### 3.2. Atonement, grace, and transformation

**Objectives**

- Explain grace as more than a reward after effort.
- Connect redemption with transformation without a simplistic payment formula.

**Learning items**

- Alma 7 links Christ’s suffering with his capacity to succor.
- Redemption is grounded in Christ rather than independent human sufficiency.
- Forgiveness, resurrection, and transformation should be explained distinctly.

**Original Hearken article · 20 XP**

An account of grace becomes distorted when divine help is treated as a prize for already being good enough. Read texts about mercy and reconciliation before constructing an effort-versus-grace formula. Latter-day Saint teaching includes both dependence on Christ and invitations to act; understanding their relationship requires attention to the passage’s purpose. A response to grace is not necessarily a competing source of salvation. Also distinguish forgiveness, resurrection, healing, and sanctification rather than using “Atonement” as an unexplained answer to every question. A fictional pastoral example can reveal a hidden assumption: what would you say to someone who believes one failure makes divine help unavailable? Support your answer with a passage, and avoid claiming to determine that person’s standing before God.

### 3.3. Baptism, confirmation, and covenant responsibility

**Objectives**

- Explain an ordinance’s communal and individual dimensions.
- Separate ordinance descriptions from an exhaustive worthiness policy.

**Learning items**

- Confirmation and the gift of the Holy Ghost belong in an account of entry into covenant life.

Ordinances are embodied actions situated within covenant relationships and a community. Study what a passage says participants promise, receive, or become responsible for. Mosiah’s account places mutual care beside baptism, showing why a purely individual explanation is incomplete. Other passages emphasize authorized administration and reception of the Holy Ghost. These emphases can be held together without making every scriptural description a procedural handbook. For current procedures, consult current institutional instructions rather than inventing a rule from one narrative. Explain the difference between a covenant’s theological meaning and its administrative requirements. In the course, understanding is demonstrated through interpretation and a case study, not through receiving an ordinance or revealing one’s membership status.

### 3.4. Revelation, discernment, and stewardship

**Objectives**

- Distinguish personal guidance from authority to direct others.
- Use intellectual and spiritual inquiry without a mechanical certainty test.

**Learning items**

- Revelatory language must be read in context.
- Study and inquiry are part of the instruction in D&C 9.
- Personal conviction does not by itself establish authority over another person.

**Original Hearken article · 20 XP**

Revelation is a theological claim about divine communication, and discernment concerns how people interpret what they believe they have received. A feeling should not become an automatic warrant to control someone else. Study the connection between inquiry, preparation, and reflection, while recognizing that a course cannot verify the divine origin of an individual experience. Stewardship helps define the limits of a claim: guidance for one’s own responsibilities differs from a binding instruction for a community. When sources appear to conflict, slow down. Identify the exact claim, consult appropriate sources, consider alternative explanations, and avoid rushing into consequential action. Learning how a tradition understands revelation is compatible with uncertainty and with respecting a learner who interprets spiritual experience differently.

### 4.1. Priesthood, keys, authority, and service

**Objectives**

- Distinguish priesthood power, authority, office, and keys.
- Explain service without equating office with personal superiority.

**Learning items**

- Keys, office, authority, and power are related but distinct terms.
- D&C 121 limits coercive claims to righteous authority.
- Current administrative descriptions should be checked in current institutional sources.

**Original Hearken article · 20 XP**

Related terms should not be treated as synonyms. Priesthood discussions may concern divine power, authorization to act, an office, or the right to direct particular work. Ask which meaning is operating in each sentence. An explanation of keys is incomplete if it never explains whose responsibilities they govern. A discussion of power is incomplete if it suggests that position alone proves righteousness. Study descriptions of persuasion, patience, and love alongside accounts of organization. Current institutional guidance helps prevent a learner from making administrative claims from memory. A useful diagram connects responsibilities and service while avoiding a ladder of human worth. When discussing women and men, distinguish the Church’s current descriptions from historical practices and from a scholar’s interpretation.

### 4.2. Temple worship and covenants

**Objectives**

- Describe publicly explained temple purposes respectfully.
- Distinguish proxy participation from an assumption of forced acceptance.

**Learning items**

- Temple learning in this course uses public doctrine and descriptions.
- Covenants connect worship and discipleship.
- Proxy ordinances should be explained with agency rather than compelled acceptance.

**Original Hearken article · 20 XP**

A public curriculum can explain temple purposes through publicly available sources without reproducing restricted ceremonial details. Focus on covenant, worship, preparation, service, and the relationship between present discipleship and eternal hope. Keep procedural statements modest: temple administration can change, and a general overview is not a substitute for current instructions. Discussions of proxy ordinances should explain the theological role of individual choice rather than depicting rites as overriding someone’s agency. Sacredness also calls for respectful language toward people who do not share the teaching. Construct an explanation that answers a newcomer’s real questions, names its sources, and clearly marks the boundary between public doctrine and details the course does not need to teach.

### 8.3. Capstone counterargument: embodiment, relationships, and eternal hope

**Objectives**

**Learning items**

- A serious objection uses actual evidence rather than a caricature.
- Embodiment and eternal relationships involve more than one passage.

**Original Hearken article · 20 XP**

## Sage capstone · 200 XP

A sourced LDS doctrinal synthesis

Choose a bounded doctrinal question. Submit an essay, presentation with source notes, or an accessible equivalent meeting the same criteria.

- One bounded research question and a defensible thesis.
- At least 10 substantively used sources, including at least 4 canonical selections and 2 current LDS institutional sources.
- At least 1 historical primary source and 1 source representing a comparison tradition when relevant to the chosen question; otherwise explain why they are not relevant and substitute an appropriate academic or documentary source.
- Identify perspective and claim type; do not treat an institutional essay as independent scholarly consensus.
- One serious alternative reading or objection, answered or used to narrow the thesis.
- One fictional practical or teaching application.
- A claim-level citation trail, annotated bibliography, revision log, accessible newcomer summary, and at least 3 explicit limitations or open questions.
- Use only original writing, approved app scripture rendering, links, and rights-cleared materials.

**Rubric:** accuracy/context, traceable sources, fair alternative, and coherent synthesis/limitations. Each criterion is scored 0–3. Pass at 9/12 with no zero. A qualified human reviewer or a validated assessment workflow must evaluate the rubric. Self-checking completion does not constitute a pass. A course facilitator should have documented familiarity with LDS doctrine, source criticism, and respectful cross-tradition comparison.

Possible questions: How do selected LDS texts connect grace and agency? How does LDS covenant theology connect worship and communal responsibility? What can a source-based account of eternal hope say, and what remains unresolved? How should a doctrine be taught after its historical development complicates a simple summary?

Complete the final stage check as well as the capstone to earn Sage. Sage is a learning achievement; it confers no religious office or spiritual status.
