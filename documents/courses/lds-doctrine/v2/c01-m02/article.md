# Making careful claims

## From source to sentence

In the last module you sorted sources into four kinds: canonical scripture, institutional teaching, commentary, and historical records. Sorting is the first half of careful study. The second half happens when you write. Every time you put a teaching into your own words, you make a claim, and a claim can say more or less than its source supports.

This module is about that second half. Its goal is simple to state and takes practice to do: every doctrinal claim you write should name its source and say where that source's support ends. A claim that does both is not timid. It is strong in exactly the way that matters, because anyone who checks it will find it true.

You will practice on one of the most important texts in Latter-day Saint scripture, Joseph Smith's account of his first vision, and on the fact that he described that experience more than once. Several accounts of one event are a natural test of careful claims. They reward the reader who notices which account says what, and they trip up the reader who blends them together.

## The four fields at work

The source ledger from module 1.1 has four fields: claim, source, setting and confidence. Here is how they work on a real passage.

Take Joseph Smith—History 1:17, where Joseph describes seeing "two Personages, whose brightness and glory defy all description." One of them, he writes, called him by name and said, pointing to the other, "This is My Beloved Son. Hear Him!"

**Claim.** In this account, Joseph Smith says he saw two Personages, and that one introduced the other as his Beloved Son.

**Source.** Canonical scripture: Joseph Smith—History 1:17, in the Pearl of Great Price.

**Setting.** Joseph tells us the setting himself. Verse 1 says he wrote to answer "the many reports" circulating about the Church, and verse 2 dates the writing to 1838, "the eighth year since the organization" of the Church. The event itself, verse 14 says, happened "early in the spring of eighteen hundred and twenty."

**Confidence.** High that this is what the canonized account says. The ledger entry also tells you what kind of claim you can build on it: a claim about the account. To write about the event in general, as other accounts describe it, you would need those accounts too.

Notice how much the setting field adds. An account written in 1838 about an event in 1820, intended for a public that had heard hostile reports, is a particular kind of document. Knowing that does not make it less important. It tells you how to cite it precisely.

## Three common slips

Most overstatements fall into a few patterns. Learning to spot them in your own writing is more useful than memorizing rules.

**From one passage to everyone.** "This passage teaches X" becomes "Latter-day Saints believe X." The second sentence is a claim about millions of people, and a passage can't support it by itself. Institutional teaching can tell you what the Church teaches. Only evidence about members can tell you what members believe, and even then it rarely covers all of them.

**From one moment to always.** "This 1880 sermon says X" becomes "The Church teaches X." The first sentence is about a document with a date. The second is about the present. Module 1.1 showed why the gap matters: the Church's guide expects teachings and emphasis to develop, and a single statement on one occasion may be a personal view.

**From one speaker to the institution.** "Elder Y said X" becomes "Mormon doctrine says X." Who said something matters, but a speaker's title alone does not turn a statement into doctrine. The councils and sources named in the Church's guide do that work.

The repair for each slip is the same: put the source back into the sentence. "In the 1838 account…", "A current Church explanation says…", "One nineteenth-century sermon claimed…". Each of these is shorter than the hedge it replaces, and more informative.

## Reading a narrative carefully

Joseph Smith—History 1:1–20 is a story, and stories need their own kind of care. A narrative makes claims through events, speech and the narrator's comments, and these are not all the same kind of claim.

Some verses report circumstances. Verse 5 describes "an unusual excitement on the subject of religion" in the area around Manchester, beginning with the Methodists. Verses 7 and 8 describe Joseph's family and his own uncertainty: he leaned toward the Methodists but could not decide "who was right and who was wrong."

Some verses report Joseph's inner life. Verse 12 says no passage of scripture ever came "with more power to the heart of man" than James 1:5 did to his. That is a statement about his experience, and it is the kind of thing only the narrator can report.

Some verses report what was said to him. In verses 18 and 19 Joseph asks which church to join and is told to join none of them. The Personage, he writes, said "all their creeds were an abomination in his sight," and quoted words about drawing near "with their lips" while hearts are "far from me."

When you cite verse 19, the careful form names who is speaking in the account and what is being described: in Joseph Smith's 1838 account, the Personage condemns the creeds and their "professors" in answer to Joseph's question about which church to join. Two slips are common here. One drops the attribution and turns the verse into a general statement. The other stretches it into a claim the verse does not make, such as a claim about what Latter-day Saints today think of every individual Christian. For that, you would need other sources, and you would cite them.

The narrator's comments are a fourth kind of claim. Verse 20 closes with Joseph's own reflection that "the adversary was aware" early in his life that he would be "a disturber and an annoyer of his kingdom." That is Joseph's interpretation of his experience, offered as his.

## When there are several accounts

Joseph Smith described the First Vision more than once. The Church's page on First Vision accounts says he left four accounts of his own, and that five of his contemporaries also wrote accounts after hearing him describe it. He published two of his accounts; the best known is the one canonized in the Pearl of Great Price that you are reading in this module. The other two, his earliest written account from 1832 and a journal entry, were not published in his lifetime. The Church published them in the 1960s. All of the accounts were written more than a decade after the event.

The accounts agree in their center. The Church's page notes that they tell a consistent story and all emphasize Jesus Christ, who answers Joseph's questions. They also differ in ways a careful reader should notice.

- **Why he prayed.** The 1832 account says Joseph sought forgiveness of his sins. Later accounts emphasize his question about which church to join. The page describes these concerns as closely related in his mind.
- **Who appeared.** The 1832 account refers to "the Lord." Later accounts describe two Personages, God the Father and Jesus Christ, and one also mentions angels.
- **What was said.** The introduction "This is My Beloved Son" appears in the canonized account but not in the 1832 one.

The Church's page offers explanations of its own. People emphasize different parts of an experience for different audiences and purposes, and the accounts were written for different settings: a private history, a visitor, an official history and a newspaper editor's inquiry. Joseph's understanding of the vision's meaning also grew over time. The page adds that historians expect this kind of variation when an experience is retold from memory, and it compares the multiple New Testament accounts of Paul's conversion.

For a careful student the practical lesson is about wording. A claim about the First Vision should say which account it draws on whenever the accounts differ. "In the 1838 account, Joseph describes two Personages" is precise. "Joseph always said he saw two beings" is not, because the earliest account says something different. Where the accounts agree, you can say so: "Every account centers on Jesus Christ answering Joseph's prayer."

## Quoting in context

A quotation carries its context with it whether or not you include it. Leaving context out does not make a quote neutral. It quietly substitutes a new one.

Three habits keep quotations honest. First, quote enough to keep the meaning. If a verse begins with a condition, quote the condition. Second, say who is speaking within the text. A narrator, a character, a quoted prophet and a voice from heaven are different speakers, and scripture often contains all four. Third, keep the question the text was answering. Joseph's question in verse 18 was which church to join, and the answer in verse 19 answers that question.

The same habits apply in the other direction. If someone quotes a passage at you, ask what comes before and after it, who is speaking, and what question it answers. You will often find that the passage supports a narrower claim than the one built on it, and occasionally a broader one.

## Writing with the right confidence

Careful writing is not hedged writing. A sentence full of "perhaps" and "some might say" tells the reader nothing about where the evidence actually is. Precise verbs do that work better.

- **"States" or "says"** fits when the source says it in so many words. Article of Faith 9 states that God "will yet reveal many great and important things."
- **"Teaches"** fits when a source presents something as doctrine, especially canonical or institutional sources.
- **"Describes"** fits a narrative: Joseph Smith—History 1 describes a pillar of light.
- **"Suggests" or "implies"** marks your inference, and tells the reader to check your reasoning.
- **"Some members believe"** needs evidence about members, not about doctrine.

Here is a rewrite that puts the module together. The overbroad version: "Mormons believe God told Joseph Smith that all other churches are evil." The careful version: "In his 1838 account, canonized in Joseph Smith—History 1:19, Joseph Smith writes that he was told to join none of the churches of his day, and that the Personage called their creeds an abomination; earlier accounts describe the vision differently." The careful version is longer, but every word in it can be checked, and none of it claims more than the source shows.
