# Sources and authority

## Why the kind of source matters

Suppose a friend tells you, "Latter-day Saints believe that God lives near a star called Kolob." Another friend says, "Latter-day Saints believe that Jesus Christ is the Savior of the world." Both sentences begin the same way, and both can be traced to something written by Latter-day Saints. Yet they are not the same kind of claim. The second names the center of the faith. The first draws on a short passage in the Book of Abraham (Abraham 3) whose meaning Church members discuss in different ways, and which is not a focus of the Church's teaching today.

Most confusion about Latter-day Saint doctrine, from members and observers alike, starts here. A sentence is quoted, the quote is real, and the conclusion drawn from it is much larger than the source can carry. The remedy is not suspicion of every source. It is learning to ask, every time, what kind of source this is and what it can actually show.

This module gives you four categories to work with: canonical scripture, institutional teaching, commentary, and historical records. They are not a ladder where the top rung wins every argument. They are different tools that answer different questions. By the end of the module you should be able to place a source in its category, say what it can and cannot establish, and record it in a way that keeps your later claims honest.

A word about what this course measures. You will be assessed on whether you can describe Latter-day Saint teaching accurately and use sources carefully. You will never be asked whether you believe it. A careful explanation is a skill, and it is the same skill whether you are a lifelong member, a curious neighbor, or a scholar of religion.

## The standard works: an open canon

The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints recognizes four books of scripture, called the standard works: the Bible, the Book of Mormon, the Doctrine and Covenants, and the Pearl of Great Price. Together they form the canon, the collection the Church accepts as scripture for the whole body of members. When a lesson in this course says a teaching is "canonical," it means the teaching appears in one of these four books.

Each book has its own character. The Bible is shared with other Christians, and Latter-day Saints in English have long used the King James Version. The Book of Mormon presents itself as a record of ancient peoples in the Americas and of Christ's ministry among them. The Doctrine and Covenants gathers revelations and inspired documents, most of them from Joseph Smith's lifetime. The Pearl of Great Price is the smallest. It contains the Book of Moses, the Book of Abraham, Joseph Smith—Matthew, Joseph Smith—History, and the thirteen Articles of Faith you will read in this module.

Two of those articles describe how the canon itself is understood. Article of Faith 8 accepts the Bible "as far as it is translated correctly," and the Book of Mormon as the word of God. Article of Faith 9 reaches forward: the Church believes "all that God has revealed, all that He does now reveal," and that God "will yet reveal many great and important things." This is what an open canon means. The collection is not assumed to be finished.

The canon has in fact grown in living memory. In 1976 two revelations were accepted into scripture by the vote of a general conference, and in 1981 they became sections 137 and 138 of the Doctrine and Covenants. In 1978 the announcement known as Official Declaration 2 was presented to a general conference and accepted the same way. Additions to the canon are rare, formal, and presented to the membership. That is part of what makes the standard works a stable starting point. When you cite a passage from them, anyone can check it, and its status is not in dispute.

Canonical does not mean self-interpreting, though. A verse can be quoted accurately and still be stretched. The Kolob example above is canonical text, but the claim built on it is an interpretation. Keeping those two apart, the words of the passage and the reading you give it, is the first habit of careful study.

## Institutional teaching: how the Church speaks today

Scripture is not the only way the Church teaches. Members are taught each week from Church-published manuals, and twice a year, in April and October, general conference brings addresses from Church leaders that are published afterward. The Church's website hosts Gospel Topics pages and essays that explain teachings and history. There are formal documents too, such as "The Family: A Proclamation to the World," issued in 1995.

Who decides what the Church teaches? The Church's own guide, "Approaching Latter-day Saint Doctrine," published by its newsroom in 2007, gives a direct answer. Doctrine is established by the First Presidency and the Quorum of the Twelve Apostles, who counsel together, and it is consistently proclaimed in official Church publications. The guide names where doctrine is found: the four standard works, official declarations and proclamations, and the Articles of Faith.

The guide also says something that surprises many readers. Not every statement made by a Church leader, past or present, is doctrine. A single statement by one leader on a single occasion often represents a personal, though considered, opinion. It is not meant to be binding on the whole Church. The guide warns that isolated statements are often taken out of context and given a weight they were never meant to carry.

How does that fit with scripture? Doctrine and Covenants 68 is often quoted here. Its first verses were addressed to Orson Hyde and offered as an "ensample" to others ordained to the priesthood. Verse 4 says that "whatsoever they shall speak when moved upon by the Holy Ghost shall be scripture." Latter-day Saints read this as a description of inspired teaching. Notice what the verse does and does not say. It ties scripture in this broad sense to the influence of the Holy Ghost. It does not say every sermon is canon, and it says nothing about adding words to the standard works. The guide and the verse together point the same way: authority comes from inspiration and from the councils that establish doctrine, not from a speaker's title alone.

So institutional teaching is a category of its own. A current Gospel Topics page or an address published by the Church shows how the Church teaches and explains something today. That is valuable evidence. It is not the same as canon, and an older statement by one leader is not the same as either.

## Commentary: useful, but someone's reading

A great deal of what people read about Latter-day Saint belief is commentary: books, podcasts, articles, classes and websites in which an author explains, defends, criticizes or applies the faith. Some commentary is excellent. Some is published by authors who held high Church office. Some of it is the most helpful reading you will find on a topic.

Commentary's defining feature is that it is one person's reading. An author may quote scripture or institutional sources accurately, but the argument the author builds from them belongs to the author. Quoting an Article of Faith does not turn a devotional book into scripture, any more than quoting the Constitution turns a newspaper column into law.

Consider James E. Talmage's *The Articles of Faith*, first published in 1899. Talmage later served as an apostle, and the book was influential for generations. It is still worth reading. But it is historical commentary: it shows how a learned Latter-day Saint explained the faith in his era. It does not settle what the Church teaches now. When you cite it, say so: "Talmage argued in 1899 that…" is accurate. "The Church teaches that…" would need a different source.

The working rule for commentary is simple. Name the author, name the date, and describe the claim as theirs until you find canonical or institutional support for it. Popularity does not change the category. A book can be quoted a million times and still be one author's interpretation.

## Historical records: what someone said, when

The fourth category is historical evidence: journals and diaries, meeting minutes, letters, newspaper reports, early printed sermons. Latter-day Saints have kept records from the beginning. Doctrine and Covenants 21:1 opens with the command that "there shall be a record kept among you." As a result, the historical record is unusually rich.

Historical sources answer historical questions. A diary entry from 1880 can show what its writer heard, or believed they heard, at a particular meeting. It can show that an idea was being discussed in a certain place and time. It cannot by itself show that the idea was the Church's doctrine then, and it says nothing at all about what the Church teaches today.

Records also need their own care. Was the writer present, or reporting what someone else said? Was the entry written that evening or years later? Do other sources from the same meeting agree? These are the same questions a historian asks of any document, and they apply with full force to religious history.

Historical records matter most when wording and emphasis change over time. The Church's guide says plainly that it does not rule out future additions or changes to its teachings or practices. A careful student does not hide such changes or treat them as scandals. They date each source and let the dates speak.

## Central and peripheral teachings

Not all teachings carry the same weight. The guide makes this point with a contrast. Some doctrines are more important than others and might be called core doctrines. The guide names the Atonement of Jesus Christ as an example. Others are peripheral. Its example is the location of the Garden of Eden, a subject on which members may hold varying views and which commentators sometimes wrongly treat as central.

The guide also recalls Joseph Smith's own summary. The testimony of the apostles and prophets concerning Christ's death, burial and resurrection is the foundation of the religion. All other things, he said, are only appendages to it.

This distinction protects you from two opposite errors. One is treating an obscure passage as if it defined the faith. The other is dismissing a central teaching because you have found an odd statement elsewhere. When you study a topic, ask where it sits. Is it named in the standard works and taught consistently in current Church publications? Or does it rest on a single passage, a single sermon, or a commentator's speculation?

Watch the vocabulary too. The guide notes that some Latter-day Saint terms carry meanings different from those in other traditions. Its example is being "born again," which Latter-day Saints tend to describe as a process rather than a single moment. Many disagreements turn out to be differences in definition. Defining your terms from the tradition's own sources is part of using a source well.

## The source ledger

The habits in this module come together in a simple tool you will use for the rest of the course: the source ledger. Every time you take a note on a teaching, record four things beside it.

**Claim.** What exactly does the source say? Quote it or paraphrase it closely, in the source's own terms.

**Source.** Which kind is it: canonical scripture, institutional teaching, commentary, or a historical record? Give the full reference, such as book, chapter and verse, or title, author and date.

**Setting.** Who said or wrote it, when, to whom, and for what purpose? A revelation addressed to one person, a conference address to the whole Church, and a private letter are different settings.

**Confidence.** How far does this source carry the claim you want to make? Write the limit down.

Here is a worked example. You want to write that Latter-day Saints expect further revelation.

Claim: "We believe that He will yet reveal many great and important things pertaining to the Kingdom of God."

Source: canonical scripture, Article of Faith 9, Pearl of Great Price.

Setting: one of thirteen statements of belief written by Joseph Smith in 1842, now part of the canon.

Confidence: high that the Church affirms an expectation of further revelation. It does not say what will be revealed, or when.

From that ledger entry you can write a sentence that is both strong and accurate: "Article of Faith 9 affirms that God will yet reveal 'many great and important things'; it does not specify what those will be." Compare that with "Mormons think the Church can change anything at any time." That sentence is broader than any source in this module supports.

Start your own ledger as you work through the readings. You will revise entries as you learn more, and in later courses you will be glad of the habit. The aim is not to hedge every sentence. It is to make every sentence say exactly as much as its source allows.
