# Unity language and the Nicene comparison

## Comparing fairly

Latter-day Saint teaching about the Godhead is often explained by contrast with the doctrine of the Trinity held by Catholic, Orthodox and most Protestant Christians. Contrast can be clarifying. It can also go badly wrong, because each tradition uses ordinary words like "one," "person" and "being" in specialized ways, and it is easy to describe the other side in terms it would never use for itself.

This module sets a standard for comparison that you will use throughout the course: describe each tradition so that its own members would recognize the description as fair. You will read a Book of Mormon chapter that speaks of the Father, Son and Holy Ghost as one, and the Catholic Church's own account of the Trinity in the Catechism of the Catholic Church. Then you will compare them carefully, noting where they really differ and where the differences are only apparent.

As always in this course, you are assessed on accuracy, not agreement. A Latter-day Saint who can explain the Trinity the way a Catholic theologian would, and a Catholic who can explain the Godhead the way a Latter-day Saint teacher would, have both learned something valuable.

## "One" in 3 Nephi 11

Third Nephi 11 describes the risen Jesus Christ appearing to people gathered at a temple in the land Bountiful. It is one of the Book of Mormon's central chapters, and it uses the language of oneness several times.

The chapter opens with a voice from heaven: "Behold my Beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased" (verse 7). Christ then descends and introduces himself (verse 10). Later, giving instructions for baptism, he says it should be done "in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost" (verse 25), and adds: "the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Ghost are one; and I am in the Father, and the Father in me, and the Father and I are one" (verse 27). Verse 36 repeats it: "the Father, and I, and the Holy Ghost are one."

The same chapter keeps the three distinct in how it speaks. The Father's voice introduces the Son. The Son says he has "suffered the will of the Father" (verse 11). The Holy Ghost bears record of the Father and the Son (verse 32). Latter-day Saint readers take the chapter's oneness language as unity of purpose and witness among distinct beings, consistent with what you studied in module 1.3. That is the tradition's reading of its own scripture, and you can describe it as such.

Notice also what the chapter says about disputes. Christ tells the people that "there shall be no disputations among you" about baptism or "the points of my doctrine" (verse 28), and that "the spirit of contention" is not of him (verse 29). That is a fitting text to keep in mind when comparing beliefs.

## The Trinity in its own words

The Catechism of the Catholic Church, the Catholic Church's official summary of its teaching, treats the Trinity in paragraphs 232 through 267. Several of its points are essential to describing the doctrine accurately.

The Trinity is called "the central mystery of Christian faith and life" (paragraph 234). The Catechism teaches that it is a mystery in a strict sense: something that could not be known unless God revealed it, and that human reason cannot fully grasp even after it is revealed (paragraph 237).

The Church confesses "one God in three persons" (paragraph 253). It does not confess three Gods. Each of the persons is fully God, and they do not divide the one divinity among themselves.

To say this precisely, the Catechism uses three technical terms (paragraph 252). **Substance**, also called essence or nature, names the divine being in its unity. **Person**, or hypostasis, names the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit in their real distinction from one another. **Relation** names the way that distinction consists in their relationships to one another: the Father begets, the Son is begotten, the Spirit proceeds.

Finally, the persons are really distinct. They are not simply three ways the one God appears (paragraph 254). The Catechism also traces this teaching to the early councils: the Council of Nicaea in 325 confessed that the Son is "consubstantial" with the Father, and the Council of Constantinople in 381 confessed the Holy Spirit as "the Lord and giver of life" (paragraphs 242 and 245). The creed associated with these councils is usually called the Nicene Creed.

## Three caricatures to avoid

With those points in hand, you can recognize three common misdescriptions of the Trinity. Each one is rejected by the Catechism itself.

**"One God wearing three masks."** This view, that the Father, Son and Spirit are three modes or appearances of a single person, has a name: modalism. The Catechism rejects it when it says the persons are really distinct and not mere modes of the divine being (paragraph 254).

**"Three gods."** Some critics describe the Trinity as three gods with a single label. The Catechism explicitly says the Church does not confess three Gods but one God in three persons (paragraph 253).

**"The Son is a lesser, created being."** The councils the Catechism cites taught the opposite: the Son is consubstantial with the Father.

The same care runs both ways. Latter-day Saints are sometimes caricatured too, for example as not believing in Jesus Christ, when Article of Faith 1 and the scriptures in this module center on him. A fair comparison avoids both kinds of shortcut.

## Where the traditions really differ

Once the caricatures are cleared away, a real difference remains, and it is worth stating clearly.

Catholic teaching describes the Father, Son and Holy Spirit as three distinct persons who share one divine substance: one God. Latter-day Saint teaching describes the Father, Son and Holy Ghost as three distinct beings who are one in purpose. In Latter-day Saint sources, the Father and the Son each have a body of flesh and bones (Doctrine and Covenants 130:22).

So both traditions affirm that the three are distinct, and both affirm that they are one. The difference lies in what kind of oneness each affirms. For Catholic teaching, the oneness is a single divine substance. For Latter-day Saint teaching, it is unity of purpose among separate beings. That is a substantial difference, and neither side treats it as a misunderstanding of words. Stating it this way respects both.

## Same words, different meanings

Several words appear in both traditions with different meanings. A two-column glossary, as suggested in module 1.1, prevents most errors.

**One.** In the Catechism, the oneness of God is the unity of the divine substance. In Latter-day Saint sources, the oneness of the Godhead is unity of purpose and doctrine among distinct beings.

**Person.** In the Catechism, a technical term for the Father, Son and Spirit in their distinction, defined by relation. In Latter-day Saint usage, "personage" in scripture such as Doctrine and Covenants 130:22 refers to an individual being, embodied or a spirit.

**Being.** Latter-day Saint sources speak of three separate beings. Catholic teaching speaks of one divine being in three persons.

**God.** Both traditions use it of the Father in particular and of the divine as a whole. Check which use a source makes before you compare sentences.

When a sentence from one tradition is read with the other tradition's definitions, it can seem to say something absurd. That is a signal to go back to the glossary, not a discovery about the other side.

## Writing a fair comparison

A fair comparison follows a few steps.

1. Describe each tradition from its own authoritative sources: here, the Catechism for Catholic teaching and the standard works and Church publications for Latter-day Saint teaching.
2. Define the shared words separately for each tradition.
3. State what both affirm before stating where they differ.
4. Name the real difference in terms each side would accept.
5. Avoid saying what the other tradition "really" means beyond what its sources say.

Here is a short example. "Both Catholic and Latter-day Saint teaching name the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit and affirm that they are both distinct and one. The Catechism teaches one God in three persons, distinct in their relations but sharing one divine substance. Latter-day Saint sources teach three separate beings, the Father and Son embodied, who are one in purpose. The Catechism rejects describing the Trinity as either three gods or one person in three roles."

Every claim in that paragraph can be checked against the readings in this module, and a member of either tradition could read it without feeling misrepresented.
