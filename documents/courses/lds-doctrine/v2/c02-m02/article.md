# Agency and the Fall

## Why agency comes first

In module 2.1 you read that Satan "sought to destroy the agency of man" (Moses 4:3). In Latter-day Saint scripture, the conflict at the heart of the premortal council was not about whether people would be saved but about whether they would choose. This module asks why agency matters so much in Latter-day Saint teaching, and how it connects to one of the most distinctive Latter-day Saint teachings of all: that the Fall of Adam and Eve was a necessary step in God's plan, not simply a catastrophe.

Your main text is 2 Nephi 2, a father's blessing to his son. Lehi, near the end of his life, speaks to his son Jacob about redemption, opposition, choice and the Fall. It is one of the densest theological chapters in the Book of Mormon, and it rewards slow reading. You will also read Moses 4:1–4 and 5:9–12, which give the premortal setting of agency and Adam and Eve's own response to their fall.

## Opposition in all things

The heart of Lehi's argument is a claim about the structure of the world: "it must needs be, that there is an opposition in all things" (2 Nephi 2:11). Without opposition, he says, "righteousness could not be brought to pass, neither wickedness, neither holiness nor misery, neither good nor bad."

Lehi then builds a chain of reasoning in verse 13. If there is no law, there is no sin; if no sin, no righteousness; if no righteousness, no happiness; and so on, until "if these things are not there is no God." The argument runs from the existence of moral alternatives to the meaning of the whole creation. Its point is that good and evil, happiness and misery, are only meaningful in a world where both are possible.

When you summarize this, keep it attributed and keep it tied to its purpose. Lehi is not giving a general theory of physics. He is explaining why a world with real moral choice, and therefore real possibility of sin, is necessary to God's "eternal purposes."

## To act and not to be acted upon

From opposition, Lehi moves to agency. God created "things to act and things to be acted upon" (verse 14). He set "the forbidden fruit in opposition to the tree of life" (verse 15), and "gave unto man that he should act for himself" (verse 16). Then comes a striking qualification: "man could not act for himself save it should be that he was enticed by the one or the other."

Later in the chapter Lehi says that because people are redeemed from the Fall, they have "become free forever, knowing good from evil; to act for themselves and not to be acted upon" (verse 26). They are "free to choose liberty and eternal life, through the great Mediator of all men, or to choose captivity and death" (verse 27).

Two things are worth noticing. First, agency in this chapter requires real alternatives and real enticement in both directions. Second, the freedom Lehi describes is connected to Christ: it is through redemption from the Fall that people become free to choose. Agency in this teaching is not simply a natural fact. It is a gift that depends on the Redeemer.

## Agency in the premortal council

Moses 4:1–4, which you read in module 2.1, frames agency as the issue in the premortal council. Satan offered to redeem everyone, "that one soul shall not be lost," in exchange for God's honor (verse 1). The Beloved Son said, "Father, thy will be done, and the glory be thine forever" (verse 2). Satan "sought to destroy the agency of man" and was cast down (verse 3), becoming "the father of all lies, to deceive and to blind men, and to lead them captive at his will" (verse 4).

Read with 2 Nephi 2, the logic is consistent. A plan that guaranteed no one would be lost would have removed real choice. In Lehi's terms, it would have removed the opposition that makes righteousness and happiness possible. Lehi's description of the devil fits too: he "sought also the misery of all mankind" (2 Nephi 2:18), "that all men might be miserable like unto himself" (verse 27).

## The Fall as a step forward

The most distinctive part of 2 Nephi 2 is its account of the Fall. Lehi says that if Adam had not transgressed, he and Eve "would have remained in the garden of Eden," and all things "must have remained in the same state" (verse 22). "They would have had no children; wherefore they would have remained in a state of innocence, having no joy, for they knew no misery; doing no good, for they knew no sin" (verse 23).

Then come two famous sentences: "all things have been done in the wisdom of him who knoweth all things" (verse 24), and "Adam fell that men might be; and men are, that they might have joy" (verse 25).

Moses 5 gives Adam and Eve's own reaction. After the Holy Ghost bore witness to Adam that "as thou hast fallen thou mayest be redeemed" (verse 9), Adam rejoiced: "because of my transgression my eyes are opened, and in this life I shall have joy, and again in the flesh I shall see God" (verse 10). Eve said, "Were it not for our transgression we never should have had seed, and never should have known good and evil, and the joy of our redemption, and the eternal life which God giveth unto all the obedient" (verse 11).

The picture these passages give is consistent. The Fall brought mortality, children, knowledge of good and evil, and the need for redemption, and Adam and Eve themselves are portrayed as glad of it once they understand the plan. Notice the vocabulary: these passages speak of Adam's "transgression," and Lehi says Adam "fell." Latter-day Saint teaching often uses "transgression" for the Fall, and you should quote the passages' own words when you describe it.

## Responsibility: Article of Faith 2

If the Fall was part of the plan, are people responsible for it? Article of Faith 2 answers directly: "men will be punished for their own sins, and not for Adam's transgression."

Read that sentence for exactly what it says. It denies that anyone is punished for Adam's transgression. It affirms that people are accountable for their own sins. It does not say that the Fall had no effects. 2 Nephi 2 and Alma 42 describe real effects: mortality, separation from God's presence, and a fallen condition from which people must be redeemed (Alma 42:6–9). The article distinguishes consequences of the Fall from guilt for it.

This is a point where Latter-day Saint teaching is often compared with traditions that teach some form of original sin. A fair comparison requires reading those traditions in their own sources, which this course does in a later module. For now, describe the Latter-day Saint teaching accurately: people are not punished for Adam's transgression, and they are accountable for their own sins.

## Redemption from the Fall

Lehi's chapter is not mainly about the Fall. It is about the Redeemer. "Redemption cometh in and through the Holy Messiah; for he is full of grace and truth," Lehi says (verse 6). "He offereth himself a sacrifice for sin, to answer the ends of the law, unto all those who have a broken heart and a contrite spirit" (verse 7). No one, he says, can "dwell in the presence of God, save it be through the merits, and mercy, and grace of the Holy Messiah" (verse 8).

And again, near the end: "the Messiah cometh in the fulness of time, that he may redeem the children of men from the fall" (verse 26). The Fall and the Atonement belong together in this teaching. To describe the Fall without the redemption it was meant to be answered by is to describe half the doctrine.

## Describing it well

A good short explanation of this module's teaching keeps three things together: agency, the Fall and redemption. For example:

"In 2 Nephi 2, Lehi teaches that God gave people the power to 'act for themselves,' which requires real alternatives, or 'opposition in all things.' Latter-day Saint scripture describes the Fall as a necessary step: without it, Adam and Eve 'would have had no children' (2 Nephi 2:23), and 'Adam fell that men might be' (verse 25). Article of Faith 2 adds that people are punished for their own sins, not for Adam's transgression. The same chapter centers on Christ, who redeems people from the Fall so they can choose eternal life."

Each claim there is attributed and bounded. It describes the teaching without claiming to describe every member's view, and without describing other traditions it hasn't read.
