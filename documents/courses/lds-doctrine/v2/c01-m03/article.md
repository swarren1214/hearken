# The Godhead: three distinct beings

## Three named in one sentence

The first Article of Faith is a single sentence: "We believe in God, the Eternal Father, and in His Son, Jesus Christ, and in the Holy Ghost." Every Christian tradition names these three. What a tradition says about how they relate to one another is where the differences begin, and Latter-day Saint teaching has a distinctive answer.

Latter-day Saints use the word **Godhead** for the Father, the Son and the Holy Ghost together. The Church's Gospel Topics page on the Godhead says they are three separate beings, with distinct roles, who are one in purpose and doctrine. This module works through the sources behind each part of that sentence: that they are distinct, what the canon says about their natures, what roles the Church assigns to each, and what "one" means in Latter-day Saint sources.

The next module compares this teaching with Nicene Christianity. For now, the aim is to describe the Latter-day Saint teaching accurately on its own terms, from its own sources, before setting it beside anything else.

## Distinct beings in the scriptures

Latter-day Saints point to scenes in which the Father, the Son and the Holy Ghost appear or act at the same time, in different ways. Reading these scenes closely is the best way to see why the teaching takes the shape it does.

The baptism of Jesus in Matthew 3:13–17 is the classic example. Jesus comes up out of the water. The heavens open, and "the Spirit of God" descends "like a dove." A voice from heaven says, "This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased." In one scene, the Son stands in the river, the Spirit descends, and the Father speaks.

Acts 7:55–56 records Stephen's vision just before his death. Full of the Holy Ghost, he "saw the glory of God, and Jesus standing on the right hand of God." Joseph Smith—History 1:17, which you read in module 1.2, describes two Personages, one introducing the other: "This is My Beloved Son. Hear Him!" In 3 Nephi 11, a voice from heaven announces "my Beloved Son" before the risen Christ descends to the people.

Latter-day Saint sources read these passages as showing three distinct beings. Other Christians read the same New Testament passages too, and they interpret them within their own teaching about God; you will see how in the next module. A careful claim here names the reading: "Latter-day Saints cite the baptism of Jesus, where the Son, the Spirit and the Father's voice appear together, as evidence that they are distinct beings."

## Bodies and spirit: Doctrine and Covenants 130

The most direct canonical statement about the natures of the Father, Son and Holy Ghost is Doctrine and Covenants 130:22: "The Father has a body of flesh and bones as tangible as man's; the Son also; but the Holy Ghost has not a body of flesh and bones, but is a personage of Spirit." The verse then gives a reason: "Were it not so, the Holy Ghost could not dwell in us." Verse 23 adds that a person may receive the Holy Ghost, and the Holy Ghost may descend upon them "and not tarry."

Section 130 is not a single revelation in the way many sections are. It collects items of instruction Joseph Smith gave at Ramus, Illinois, in April 1843, on several subjects. That setting matters when you cite it. Verse 22 is canonical, and the Church's Godhead page cites it for exactly this teaching. Other verses in the section address different matters entirely, from the nature of angels to a prophecy about South Carolina. Cite the verse that supports your claim, not the section as a whole.

Read verse 22 for what it states and what it leaves open. It states that the Father and the Son each have a tangible body of flesh and bones, and that the Holy Ghost is a personage of Spirit. It does not say how this was so before Christ's mortal life, and it does not set out a full account of divine nature. When a reader moves from "the Father has a body" to detailed claims about what that body is like, they have left the verse behind. Mark that move as interpretation if you make it.

The same section opens with a related point. Verse 1 says that when the Savior appears, "we shall see that he is a man like ourselves," and verse 2 that the "same sociality" which exists here will exist there, "coupled with eternal glory." These verses help explain why embodiment matters in Latter-day Saint thought: the teaching about God is tied to a teaching about what human beings can become. That connection belongs to later courses, but it is worth noticing where it starts.

## Roles: Father, Son and Holy Ghost

The Church's Godhead page assigns each member a role. The Father is the ultimate object of worship; Latter-day Saints pray to him in the name of Jesus Christ. The Son is the Lord and Redeemer. The Holy Ghost is the messenger and revealer of the Father and the Son.

These roles show up in the scriptures you are reading. In John 17, Jesus prays to the Father. In 3 Nephi 11:11, the risen Christ says he has "glorified the Father in taking upon me the sins of the world." In 3 Nephi 11:32 he says the Holy Ghost "beareth record of the Father and me," which fits the description of the Holy Ghost as a witness and revealer.

Be careful not to turn roles into the whole picture. The page describes the three as perfectly united in carrying out the Father's plan; it does not say the Father has no part in redemption or the Son no part in revelation. Roles describe how the sources usually speak of each member. They are not a strict division of labor.

## What "one" means in Latter-day Saint sources

Scripture also speaks of the Father, Son and Holy Ghost as one. In 3 Nephi 11:27 Christ says "the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Ghost are one," and 2 Nephi 31:21 ends Nephi's explanation of the doctrine of Christ with "the only and true doctrine of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost, which is one God, without end."

Latter-day Saint sources explain this oneness as unity of purpose and doctrine among distinct beings. The Church's Godhead page says the three are perfectly united in carrying out the Father's plan of salvation. The passage Latter-day Saints most often cite for this reading is John 17. Praying for his disciples, Jesus asks "that they all may be one; as thou, Father, art in me, and I in thee, that they also may be one in us." In verse 22 he prays "that they may be one, even as we are one."

The Latter-day Saint argument from John 17 runs like this: Jesus asks that his disciples be one in the same way that he and the Father are one, and the disciples remain distinct people, so the oneness in view must be a unity that distinct persons can share. You can state that argument accurately without endorsing it. You should also know that it is an argument about interpretation. Other Christian traditions read John 17 within their own understanding of God, and they do not draw the same conclusion.

Notice too that the word "God" can be used for the whole Godhead, as in 2 Nephi 31:21, or for the Father in particular, as in Article of Faith 1. When you quote a passage, check which use it makes.

## What the sources leave open

Part of describing a teaching accurately is knowing where its sources stop. The canonical and institutional sources in this module say a good deal: that the Father, the Son and the Holy Ghost are distinct; that the Father and the Son have bodies of flesh and bones and the Holy Ghost is a personage of Spirit; what roles each fills; and that they are one in purpose.

They say much less about other questions people naturally ask. How exactly does the Holy Ghost "dwell in us"? What is the relationship of the Godhead to the Father's other creations? Popular writing sometimes fills these gaps with confident detail. A careful student labels such detail for what it is, commentary or speculation, and keeps it separate from what the sources teach.

This is not a weakness in the teaching. Every tradition's account of God includes acknowledged limits. Being clear about them is part of explaining any of them well.

## Explaining it well

Imagine a friend with no background asks you what Latter-day Saints believe about God. A good answer is short, uses the tradition's own words, cites its sources, and defines any word that might mislead. For example:

"Latter-day Saints believe in God the Father, his Son Jesus Christ, and the Holy Ghost, and call the three together the Godhead (Article of Faith 1). They teach that these are three distinct beings: in scripture the Church cites, like Jesus's baptism in Matthew 3, the Son, the Spirit and the Father's voice appear together. According to Doctrine and Covenants 130:22, the Father and the Son have bodies of flesh and bones, while the Holy Ghost is a personage of spirit. When scripture says they are 'one,' Latter-day Saints understand that as unity of purpose, not one being; the Church's Godhead page describes them as one in purpose and doctrine. 'Godhead' here means the three together, not a shared divine substance."

That paragraph is under 120 words. Every sentence attributes its claim, and the last sentence heads off a likely misunderstanding. It also avoids describing anyone else's belief, which is the subject of the next module.
